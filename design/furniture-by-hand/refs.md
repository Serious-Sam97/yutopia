# Furniture by hand — the references

The rules and numbers for laying out the inside of six town buildings by hand. They come from real places and
trade standards: architects' clearance sheets, restaurant fixture makers, the NKBA kitchen guidelines, farm
extension services, National Register nominations for real diners and houses, and real listings. Sizes are metres
first, with US inches in brackets where the US trade number is the real one.

How to read it: each building has **the layout** (where things go in *this* plot, with coordinates), then **the
numbers** (each with its source). Where sources disagree, **Follow** says which number to use and why. Coordinates
are measured from the front-left corner looking in from the street door: x across the front, y going back.

## 1. Bar / tavern — 16 × 16 m, two storeys, shopfront glass, door in the middle

### Where the bar goes

**Rule: a long straight bar down one side wall, starting a few metres in from the front glass, back bar against
that wall, bartender's end toward the back.** That is the classic American storefront saloon: the bar is "the
strongest linear element" of the room, with the back bar's mirrored bays and the foot rail running with it
([Cornell interior types — saloon](https://intypes.cornell.edu/expanded.cfm?erID=215)). Restaurant planners want the
bar seen from the door and close to the front, because it is also where people wait
([Toast — entrance design](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/on-the-line/restaurant-entrance-design),
[RD+D — bar design](https://www.rddmag.com/design/4122-bar-design-done-right)). The other well-known plan puts the
bar across the back facing the door (Old West saloons, [Legends of America](https://www.legendsofamerica.com/saloon-photo-gallery/)).

**Follow: the side wall.** The back wall is needed for the kitchen door, the toilets, the cellar hatch and the
stair up, and the bartender's back end then opens straight onto them. The bar is still in full view from the door.

That seats about 80. A real small-town bar is smaller and busier: West Rutland, VT's bar has an occupancy permit of
35 ([Hickok & Boardman listing](https://www.hickokandboardman.com/real-estate/west-rutland/167-marble-street/4953277.html)).
If it feels empty, drop the middle column of tables and put a pool table there (not researched here).

### Upstairs

Three real uses, in order of how common they were:

1. **The owner's flat** — most common. Bar below, one-bedroom flat above: 167 Marble St, West Rutland, VT
   ([BHHS](https://global.bhhs.com/commercial-for-sale/167-Marble-Street-West-Rutland-VT-05777-347933896)); 712 Main
   St, Bennington, VT, "above the bar, a private apartment" ([Raveis](https://www.raveis.com/property/flyer/21677055));
   Wardsboro, VT, a 3-bed flat over the restaurant and bar. Ely, MN's Portage Bar housed the proprietor's family
   upstairs; Milwaukee's 1889 saloons had the tavernkeeper's flat on the second floor
   ([OnMilwaukee tied houses](https://onmilwaukee.com/articles/urban-spelunking-schlitz-tiedhouses)).
2. **A hall** (function / dance / lodge room) — the brewery "tied houses" had a hall on the top floor (same source).
3. **Rented rooms** — a rooming house over the saloon (Ely).

Storage mostly went in the **cellar** (kegs, cases, the beer cooler), not upstairs. No source showed an upstairs
used mainly for storage.

**Follow:** front half the owner's flat (living room over the street, kitchen, 2 bedrooms, bath), back half a
storeroom and office reached from the back stair. Or the whole floor a function room with its own small bar, coat
corner and toilets if the town needs a hall.

## 2. 1950s streamline diner — 18 × 14 m, one storey, door in the middle of the long front

### What real diners look like inside

- **The counter runs parallel to the long front, facing the door; the back bar is against the wall behind it;
  booths line the front windows.** Miss Lorraine Diner (RI): "a long pink Tennessee marble countertop with nineteen
  stainless steel stools … seven seating booths lining the front wall"
  ([RI Historical Preservation](https://preservation.ri.gov/press-releases/recipe-success-miss-lorraine-diner-listed-national-register-historic-places)).
  The classic plan: the counter dominates, preparation against the back wall, floor-mounted stools in front
  ([Wikipedia — Diner](https://wikipedia.com/wiki/Diner)). Miss Worcester (1948): marble counter "most of the
  length", openings at the ends for staff, 14 stools ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miss_Worcester_Diner)).
- **Size.** Lamy's Diner (Worcester, 1946): 15 × 36 ft (4.6 × 11 m), 16 stools and six 4-seat booths = 40 seats
  ([The Henry Ford](https://www.thehenryford.org/collections/explore/artifact/188375)). The big postwar ones were
  double-width: Aetna Diner, Hartford (Paramount, 1948): **50 × 32 ft (15.2 × 9.75 m)** plus a separate brick
  **kitchen annex 36 × 24 ft** at the back
  ([NR nomination](https://portal.ct.gov/-/media/DECD/Historic-Preservation/06_About_SHPO/State-Review-Board/2021_Meetings/March-26_2021/Aetna-Diner-NR.pdf)).
  Our 18 × 14 is a big postwar diner with its kitchen inside the same box.
- **Aetna's interior, as surveyed:** centred stainless double doors into a small tiled vestibule with glass-block
  side walls; a GE stainless clock over the door; the back bar along one long wall in sunburst-stamped stainless
  with glass-fronted changeable-letter menu boards above it, a stainless storage cabinet in the centre of the back
  bar and a double-door refrigerator at its end; mirrors between the windows; booths with Formica tops and stainless
  coat hooks on the posts, set between the structural columns with low privacy walls; restrooms off a corner of the
  dining room; a **double-wide opening in the centre of the back wall** to the kitchen; window signs "Booth Service
  for Ladies". Booths were why postwar diners grew: prewar ones were mostly a single row of stools (same nomination).
- **Pass-through counter, open kitchen, Pullman-style booths** are the three things everyone reads as "diner"
  ([Hospitality Design — Nickel & Diner](https://hospitalitydesign.com/projects/nickel-diner/55757)).
- **Wall-O-Matic selectors** (Seeburg jukebox remotes) on the wall at each booth and on the counter, up to six on a
  cable to the jukebox ([1stDibs](https://www.1stdibs.com/furniture/more-furniture-collectibles/collectibles-curiosities/scientific-instruments/midcentury-seeburg-styled-wall-mount-jukebox-diner-soda-shop-tune-selector/id-f_21344812)).

## 3. Small grocery / general market — 16 × 16 m, two storeys, shopfront glass, door in the middle

### Where things go

- **Keep the first 1.5–4.5 m (5–15 ft) inside the door empty** (the "decompression zone"). Shoppers don't take in
  anything there ([Smartsheet — store layout](https://www.smartsheet.com/store-layout)).
- **Shoppers turn right and go round anticlockwise** (Underhill's "invariant right"), so the right wall is the
  "power wall" (same source). **Follow:** the till on the **left** as you walk in, produce on the right. People
  finish their loop at the till by the door. (No source names "till on the left". It follows from the turn right.)
- **Aisles run front to back, at right angles to the front.** Short runs of 3–5 units keep you able to see across;
  islands about 1.37 m (54″) high can be seen over ([NRS — c-store layout](https://nrsplus.com/blog/how-to-lay-out-your-convenience-store/),
  [Coohom c-store remodel](https://www.coohom.com/article/designing-a-convenience-store-floor-plan-lessons-from-my-remodel)).
  The cashier at the front looks down every aisle.
- **Cold cases on the back wall** pull people through the whole store. A small 1950s neighbourhood grocery had its
  **meat counter at the back** ([MNopedia — Stickney's grocery](https://mnopedia.org/node/12591)). Glass-door coolers
  (a walk-in with merchandiser doors) or open multidecks.

East Calais (the first operators ran the store downstairs and lived above), the J.S. Sweeney Store, Charleston (an

## 4. Farmhouse — 14 × 12 m, two storeys of 2.8 m, door in the middle of the front

### The plan: centre hall, four rooms a floor

A two-storey, five-bay house with a central door is the New England **centre-hall ("Georgian") plan**. The hall runs
front to back on both floors with a **straight stair along one hall wall**, and there's a room in each corner. The
real example: Lewis Grout House, Brattleboro, VT ([CRJC heritage](https://crjc.org/heritage/V02-199.htm)):
- "an open-string stair ascends in a straight flight along the hall's north wall"
- two front parlours either side of the hall; behind them a living room on one side, a dining room on the other
- the kitchen in a rear ell
- upstairs, four corner bedrooms off the hall, and a little room at the front of the upstairs hall (sewing room,
  later the bathroom)

The older **centre-chimney** house has a big chimney in the middle and a tiny front entry instead of a hall
([PHMC](https://www.phmc.state.pa.us/portal/communities/agriculture/field-guide/new-england-center-chimney-house.html)).
A centre hall became possible once the central chimney mass went
([Encyclopedia.com — housing](https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/housing-1)).
The New England farm usually put the kitchen in an ell behind the house ("little house") linked to the woodshed /
carriage house and the barn: "Big house, little house, back house, barn"
([Wikipedia — Connected farm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_farm)).

**Follow:** the 14 × 12 box has no ell, so the kitchen takes a back corner, with the back door by it (toward the barn).

**Stair rules (today's code, IRC R311.7):** width ≥ 0.91 m (36″), riser ≤ 197 mm (7¾″), tread ≥ 254 mm (10″),
headroom ≥ 2.03 m (6′8″) ([Boulder residential stair guide](https://bouldercolorado.gov/media/1481/download)).
Old stairs were often steeper. Keep to the code so people climb it right.

- Period look: sink under the window ([Block Renovation](https://www.blockrenovation.com/guides/1950s-kitchen-remodels-before-after-inspiration)),

0.91–1.12 m from table edge to the sideboard/wall (NKBA seating numbers above).

**Bedrooms:**
- **1950s beds were doubles (full).** Simmons brought out the first queen and king in 1958
  ([UW digital collections](https://search.library.wisc.edu/digital/A6KZJWPA6IKCXQ8V),
  [Funding Universe — Simmons](https://www.fundinguniverse.com/company-histories/simmons-company-history/)).
  Full 1.37 × 1.91 m (54 × 75″); queen 1.52 × 2.03 (60 × 80″) ([dimensions.com](https://dimensions.com/element/full-double-bedroom-layouts),
  [queen](https://dimensions.com/element/queen-bedroom-layouts)).
- Clearance on the open sides and the foot: **0.76 m minimum, 0.91 m comfortable** (dimensions.com); 0.61–0.76 is
  quoted for full/twin beds ([H&G bedrooms](https://www.homesandgardens.com/interior-design/bedrooms/an-expert-guide-to-bedroom-clearances-measurements-and-spacing)).

  (the doors swing ~0.5–0.6 m, then you stand). Keep the door swing (0.8 m door) free.
- **Old farmhouses had few or no closets.** Clothes went in wardrobes, armoires and chifforobes. The first built-in
  closets were mid-19th-century America ([Sotheby's — storage wars](https://www.sothebys.com/en/articles/storage-wars),
  [Woodworker's Journal](https://www.woodworkersjournal.com/whats-difference-armoire-chiffarobe/)). So a wardrobe in
  every bedroom is right.

## 5. Barn (gambrel) — 24 × 18 m, two storeys (~5 m), big door on the side

### The layout
